Project report · structured covariance for Bayesian deep learning

Does a learned Gauss–Markov covariance help the Laplace approximation?

A complete account of an investigation into the Laplace Covariance Model (LCM) — the structured covariance from “Stop Marginalizing My Dreams” (arXiv:2605.11804), re-purposed from model inversion to Bayesian posterior approximation. Eleven experiments, a library fork, four bugs, and a mostly-negative result that turns out to be the interesting part.

11 experiments laplace-torch fork 15 tests 4 bugs found audited: 5 checks verdict: workshop → TMLR

The short version

LCM is a correct, cheap, near-linear structured Gaussian. It approximates the exact posterior far better than a diagonal (1.6–2.9× lower KL) at 3d parameters. But that fidelity gain does not transfer to predictive uncertainty in the way you would hope: it is invisible to marginal metrics (NLL, calibration, single-point OOD) and shows up only in joint predictions — and even there only when the variables have a known 1-D ordering and the problem is well-determined (n ≳ d).

The method’s headline novelty — that the 1-D ordering is learned — does not survive testing. Gradient descent cannot discover a latent ordering, and the kernel needs the order to be ~99.9% correct before it beats a diagonal at all. Where the order is known, LCM reduces to a classical banded / state-space-GP precision.

These conclusions were then attacked directly — five checks looking for an incorporation error or a formulation, scale or data choice that manufactured the negatives. All five failed to overturn them. Notably, a larger model or a compute cluster provably cannot help: the mismatch is representational, and it widens with scale.

Orientation

What LCM is, and why it looked promising

LCM parameterises a covariance over d variables with only 3d numbers:

A = diag(d) + diag(w) · K(a) · diag(w),   K(a)ij = exp(−|ai − aj|)

Each variable gets a scalar learned coordinate ai, and correlation decays exponentially with coordinate distance. The mathematical identity that makes it fast: this is a Matérn-½ / Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Gaussian process on a line, equivalently a Gauss–Markov (AR(1)) chain. Its precision is tridiagonal, and every operation you need — sampling, log-density, log-determinant, quadratic forms — is exact in O(d)–O(d log d) via the associated state-space model (a Kalman filter).

Why this was worth testing for Bayesian learning: a posterior over many correlated variables needs exactly those operations, and a dense covariance is intractable past ~104 parameters. LCM promised full-covariance structure at diagonal-like cost. It generalises a diagonal covariance (the w = 0 case) and is far cheaper than dense or KFAC.

How everything was measured

Every method is fit to the same trained network with the same curvature backend, differing only in covariance structure: MAP (no weight uncertainty), Diag (the structure LCM generalises), KFAC (the strong layer-structured competitor), Full (exact reference), and LCM. Each method tunes its own prior precision — and, for regression, its noise — by maximising its own marginal likelihood, so every baseline gets its best case. Fidelity experiments instead fix a common prior, to isolate the curvature approximation from hyperparameter effects.

The work

Experiments, in the order they were run

Each block follows the same anatomy: why the experiment was run, what was actually done, the outcome (measured), and the conclusion drawn. The sequence matters — later experiments exist because earlier ones failed in specific, informative ways.

Phase 1 · Build the thing
DEV

Library integration: hessian_structure="lcm"

infrastructure
Why

Building standalone would have meant re-implementing (and re-validating) the predictive and marginal-likelihood machinery. Forking laplace-torch means LCM competes with diag/KFAC/full under one interface, on equal footing.

What

A new LCMCovariance module (prefix-sum kernel matvec, Kalman + associative-scan log-determinant, AR(1) sampler, exact NLL, dense and matrix-free fitters), plus LCMLaplace and LCMLLLaplace classes. 15 tests check every fast primitive against a dense reference.

Outcome

All primitives match dense references to machine precision (kernel matvec ~4e-15; log-det; NLL vs torch MVN; sampler). Full pipeline runs end-to-end for all-weights and last-layer.

Conclusion

The implementation is correct. Everything that follows measures the model, not bugs — with four important exceptions found later (see Bugs).

Phase 2 · Evaluate it honestly
EXP 1

Posterior fidelity — how close is it to the exact posterior?

positive
Why

Before asking whether LCM helps predictions, ask whether it actually captures the posterior better than a diagonal. This is the cleanest possible test of the covariance structure itself.

What

On problems small enough that the exact full posterior is computable, fix a common prior across methods and measure KL(approx ‖ full). Four settings (regression/classification × last-layer/all-weights), AR(1)-correlated features, 3 seeds.

Outcome

See table. LCM cuts diagonal's KL by 1.6–2.9× everywhere. A correlation sweep showed diagonal degrading as curvature correlation grows (KL 57.9 → 76.7 as ρ: 0 → 0.95) while LCM stays flat (~20–30).

Conclusion

The structure is real and captures genuine off-diagonal curvature. But KFAC — which exploits layer structure — is consistently better. Ordering: KFAC < LCM < Diag.

EXP 2

UCI regression — does fidelity buy better predictions?

tie
Why

Fidelity is a means, not an end. The question that matters is predictive uncertainty on real tasks.

What

Single-hidden-layer BNN on six UCI datasets, all-weights Laplace, prior and noise tuned per method by marginal likelihood, 8 random splits. Test log-likelihood (RMSE is identical across Laplace variants — they share the MAP mean — so all signal is in the LL).

Outcome

Average rank: Full 1.79 < KFAC 2.35 < LCM 3.52 ≈ Diag 3.54 < MAP 3.79. LCM and diagonal are statistically tied. LCM's one visible edge is robustness — on yacht, diagonal collapses to −2.91 (worse than MAP) while LCM recovers to −2.40.

Conclusion

First surprise: a clearly better posterior approximation produced no better predictions. This is the finding that drove the rest of the project.

EXP 3 / 3b

Image classification — calibration and OOD detection

negative
Why

Regression might be unrepresentative. Last-layer Laplace on image models is the standard “Laplace Redux” evaluation, where structured covariance is usually reported to help.

What

Small CNNs on MNIST (OOD = FashionMNIST, 3 seeds) and CIFAR-10 (OOD = SVHN, 2 seeds). Accuracy, NLL, expected calibration error, and OOD-AUROC by predictive confidence.

Outcome

MNIST is saturated — the MAP is already calibrated (ECE 0.005), so every Laplace variant slightly worsens in-distribution metrics; LCM at least has the best OOD-AUROC among them (0.968). On CIFAR-10, KFAC/Full clearly help (NLL 0.576 → ≈0.50, ECE 0.068 → ≤0.025) but LCM is worse than diagonal (ECE 0.089 vs 0.054).

Conclusion

Not a fluke of regression. For neural-network weight posteriors, LCM's fidelity edge does not become calibration, and KFAC/Full are simply the better choice.

EXP 4 / 4b

Cost, scaling, and the mechanism behind the failure

positive + explanatory
Why

Two questions: is the efficiency claim real, and why doesn't the structure help on neural networks?

What

(a) Storage and log-determinant cost vs d. (b) An ablation fitting LCM to a real last-layer GGN versus a controlled target that is a genuine chain in scrambled order — with the learned ordering and the diagonal “nugget” switched on and off.

Outcome

Cost: 3d vs d² storage (60 000 floats vs 4·108 at d = 20 000); exact log-det where dense runs out of memory. Ablation: on the controlled chain LCM recovers the target to 1.4e-4 — but needs both the learned ordering (else 0.560) and the nugget (else 0.153). On a real GGN it plateaus at 0.66 regardless of settings (diagonal: 0.973).

Conclusion

The mechanism, isolated: the model is sound — it recovers chain structure essentially perfectly when chain structure is there. Real neural-network curvature simply is not a 1-D chain (it is Kronecker-structured, which is KFAC's home). The plateau is the data, not the parameterisation.

Phase 3 · Hunt for a regime where it wins
EXP 5

Epistemic OOD — was the metric unfair to Laplace?

negative
Why

Max-confidence OOD rewards aleatoric uncertainty, which a MAP model already has. The uncertainty a posterior adds is epistemic — so the earlier comparison may have been measuring the wrong thing.

What

Re-score CIFAR-10 vs SVHN using Monte-Carlo predictive mutual information (BALD) and predictive entropy instead of confidence.

Outcome

The metric works exactly as theory demands — MAP scores 0.500 (chance: no epistemic uncertainty), and KFAC/Full jump to 0.72. But LCM (0.624) still trails diagonal (0.640).

Conclusion

The metric was unfair — to Laplace in general, not to LCM specifically. Fixing it vindicates KFAC/Full and leaves LCM's ranking unchanged.

EXP 6

Per-layer (block-diagonal) LCM

marginal
Why

If a single global chain can't order unrelated parameters across layers, give it KFAC's block structure: one chain per layer.

What

Fit a separate LCM to each parameter tensor's GGN block; compare KL-to-full against the global chain.

Outcome

Improves the global chain by 12–22% on all-weights (e.g. 95.2 → 73.9; 406.2 → 357.1) but stays far from KFAC (28.1; 104.2). Identical for last-layer, which is a single block anyway.

Conclusion

Block structure helps a little; it does not close the gap. Blocking was never the binding constraint.

EXP 7

Change of habitat: 1-D-correlated GLMs

reframing
Why

Exp 4b said the structure is only correct for genuinely 1-D-correlated, non-Kronecker curvature. Neural-network layers are the wrong habitat by construction. Where is the right one?

What

Bayesian linear models whose features have a natural 1-D order with decaying correlation — genomics (linkage disequilibrium), spectroscopy, time-series lags. Here KFAC does not apply at all (no layers) and dense Laplace is O(d³), so LCM's only real competitor is diagonal.

Outcome

Also the scalability test: after the kernel_matvec fix, the matrix-free fit completes at every d up to 20 000 with no NaN (previously NaN for every d ≥ 5000). See the table below.

Conclusion

The right habitat identified — but on this marginal metric LCM still only ties diagonal. That set up the decisive question in Exp 8.

EXP 8 / 8c

The reframing that found the real effect: joint vs marginal

the one positive
Why

Every negative so far used a marginal metric — per-point NLL, ECE, single-point OOD. But marginal predictive uncertainty is almost insensitive to off-diagonal covariance, which is precisely what a structured model adds. The metric may have been blind to the effect all along.

What

On the 1-D-correlated GLM, compare marginal predictive log-likelihood against joint predictive log-likelihood over a batch of test points (which uses the off-diagonal predictive covariance). 10 seeds with 95% confidence intervals.

Outcome

Exactly the predicted dissociation: LCM ties (slightly trails) diagonal on marginal LL, but closes a real share of the diagonal→full gap on joint LL — 62% ± 49% (d=500), 52% ± 16% (d=1000), 23% ± 12% (d=1500).

Conclusion

The effect is real but modest and shrinking with d, and not significant at d = 500. An earlier 3-seed run suggested 70–81%; the 10-seed numbers are the trustworthy ones. This is the project's central positive finding — and it is smaller than first thought.

EXP 9

Batch active learning — cashing in the joint advantage

negative, with mechanism
Why

Joint uncertainty has an obvious application: choosing a batch of jointly-informative points requires modelling redundancy between candidates, which a diagonal cannot do. If the Exp 8 effect is real, this should be where it pays.

What

Greedy batch selection maximising the joint information gain under each posterior, then scoring the chosen batch by its true information gain under the exact posterior. Random and top-k selection as references.

Outcome

LCM fails. As a fraction of the oracle's gain above random: greedy-LCM 43–55% versus greedy-diagonal 83–90% — worse even than naive top-k. Root cause isolated: active learning is inherently low-data (n ≪ d), where the curvature is low-rank; LCM's fit residual degrades from 0.13 (n=3000) to 0.74 (n=40).

Conclusion

The joint advantage requires n ≳ d — exactly the regime active learning is not in. The most natural application of the one positive finding is unavailable.

Phase 4 · Stress-test the claim before believing it
EXP 10 / 10b

The decisive test: does the learned ordering matter?

kills the novelty
Why

The sharpest reviewer objection: on data that is already ordered, a plain banded model in the given order would match LCM, so the “learned coordinate” — its novelty over classical banded-GMRF and state-space-GP models — would be doing nothing. Note LCM takes no order as input; the ordering is the fitted coordinate, sorted internally. So the real question is whether fitting a can discover an order or only refine a good initialisation.

What

Same data in two conditions — features in natural order, and randomly permuted (order latent). Four variants: fixed natural order, fixed spectral-seriation order, learned coordinate, plus a fragility sweep that corrupts the true order by known amounts.

Outcome

Given the correct order LCM works (+58%, learned +59%). With a latent order, learned recovery is 0.028 (≈ random) and joint-LL is −84%, i.e. worse than diagonal. The fragility sweep locates a brutally sharp threshold: the chain beats diagonal at |Spearman| ≥ 0.999 and fails at 0.998. Seriation recovery is 0.31 / 0.70 / 0.99 across seeds — sometimes excellent, never reliably enough.

Conclusion

The model is permutation-equivariant — given correct coordinates it works fine — so the failure is purely that fitting a is a continuous relaxation of a discrete permutation search, and gradient descent cannot cross permutation basins. The learned ordering only works when initialised at ≈ the true order: i.e. when the order is already known, where it is unnecessary.

EXP 11

Real data — does any of this survive outside simulation?

real-data positive
Why

Every joint-UQ result so far was on synthetic AR(1) features whose curvature is chain-structured by construction. No reviewer would accept that alone.

What

Two real datasets with a genuine, known 1-D order: Tecator NIR spectroscopy (100 wavelength-ordered channels, predicting fat content) and Mauna Loa CO₂ autoregression on 64 lags. 5 splits each.

Outcome

On Tecator (n ≈ d, order known), LCM closes ~100% of the diagonal→full joint-LL gap and also beats diagonal on marginal LL. On CO₂ (n ≫ d) the full-vs-diagonal gap is negligible to begin with, so there is nothing to win. In both, LCM-learned ≈ LCM-fixed.

Conclusion

A genuine real-data confirmation of the niche — and simultaneously the clearest evidence that the learned coordinate is inert, since it exactly matches simply using the known order.

Method choices

Key decisions — and what they cost

Choices that shaped what the results can and cannot say.

Why the GGN / Fisher instead of the exact Hessian

Why. Three reasons. (1) The exact Hessian of a neural loss is generally indefinite, so H⁻¹ is not a valid covariance at all; the Generalized Gauss–Newton is positive semi-definite by construction. (2) It is the standard curvature in Laplace-for-deep-learning and the library default, so comparisons to diag/KFAC/full are apples-to-apples. (3) Decisively for this project, the GGN is a sum of rank-1 outer products of per-example gradients — exactly the form LCM's Frobenius objective consumes, which is what makes a matrix-free fit possible at all.

Impact. Near a well-fit optimum the GGN ≈ Hessian, so this is mild. But it discards the loss-curvature residual term, and the empirical Fisher (using observed labels) differs from the true Fisher (expectation over model labels). Importantly, for the GLM experiments (7–11) this is not an approximation at all — the GGN is exactly XᵀΛX — so the central positive finding rests on exact curvature.

What could be done differently. Use the exact Hessian where it is PSD (convex last layers), MC-sample labels for the true Fisher, or apply a positive-curvature correction. None would likely change conclusions that are about covariance structure rather than curvature magnitude — but it is worth a robustness check before publication.

Other choices, briefly

  • Laplace rather than variational inference. Cheapest direct route from a trained network to a swappable Gaussian covariance, and Appendix A's objective maps onto fitting the Fisher. Cost: conclusions are conditional on the Laplace frame. Fitting LCM as a variational family would test the same structure independently of the GGN.
  • Materialising the dense GGN in LCMLaplace. Deliberate correctness-first choice: subclassing FullLaplace reuses its tested sampling and predictive code and allows validation against the exact posterior. Cost: the class is capped at moderate P even though the representation is O(P). The matrix-free path exists and works; wiring it end-to-end is the clean next step.
  • Synthetic AR(1) first, real data second. The synthetic target is chain-structured by construction — the ideal case, answering “does this help at all?” before “does it help in practice?”. Reporting only the synthetic result would have been misleading, which is why Exp 11 was treated as blocking.
Engineering

Four bugs — three of which failed silently

Worth reading even if you skip everything else: the recurring failure mode in this method family is silent numerical failure in exponential primitives. Nothing raised an exception; results were simply wrong or NaN.

Bug 1

Fit initialisation scale (experiment scripts). Init was scaled for the unnormalised GGN while the matrix-free fitter targets GGN/N. This produced a false conclusion — “the matrix-free fit is weak” (0.53 vs 0.22) — that sat in the project's own write-up until it was re-examined. Correctly scaled, the two match: 0.2197 vs 0.2198.

Bug 2

Inverse-softplus overflow (library). log(expm1(x)) overflows to inf/NaN for any moderately large diagonal scale, making Adam diverge silently. Fixed with a stable threshold-based helper plus data-driven auto-initialisation on both fitters.

Bug 3

kernel_matvec overflow (library) — the worst. The O(d) prefix-sum factored exp(a_j − a_i) through a single global offset, so the growing factor overflows once the coordinate range exceeds ~709 in float64 — even though every kernel entry is ≤ 1. This depends on the range, not d. It broke precisely the scalable path, returning NaN for every d ≥ 5000. First fix (logcumsumexp) was exact but 13–20× slower when benchmarked; replaced with blocked rescaling — exact to ~1e-15, finite to range 104, ~2× cost.

Bug 4

Wrong reference in the ordering diagnostic. Recovery was measured against the inverse permutation. Verified empirically rather than by argument: freezing a = perm fits the GGN to 0.14, while argsort(perm) gives 0.98. Substantive conclusions were unaffected, but it badly mis-stated seriation's recovery — and the corrected numbers make the finding sharper, since seriation can reach 0.99 and still fall short of the 0.999 threshold.

Lesson for anyone building on this

Every one of these passed small-scale correctness tests. Scalability and numerical claims must be demonstrated at the target scale, never extrapolated — and fit loops over exponential-family primitives deserve explicit finite-value assertions.

Validation

Adversarial audit — are these results real?

A negative result is only worth reporting if it is a property of the method rather than of the person testing it. So the conclusions above were then attacked directly: was the paper's code incorporated correctly, and could a choice of formulation, architecture, data or settings have manufactured the negatives? Five independent checks. All five failed to overturn the conclusions, and two of them strengthen the central claim.

AUDIT 1–2

Was the paper's code incorporated correctly?

verified exact
Port

Ran the authors' original lcm.py and mine on identical parameters against a dense MultivariateNormal ground truth. Agreement is 0.00e+00 once one detail is matched: the paper hardcodes the nugget ε at 1e-5, my default is 1e-6. That was the entire discrepancy — the associative scan, kernel and state-space formulation all match.

Integration

LCM strictly generalises a diagonal (w = 0), so an LCM posterior with w zeroed must reproduce DiagLaplace. It does: identical log-determinant (100.048805) and marginal likelihood (−128.898438), off-diagonal exactly 0.00e+00, predictions agreeing to 8.9e-08.

Conclusion

Together with the 15 tests checking every primitive against dense references, the negatives are not an implementation artifact.

AUDIT 3

Did the formulation choice manufacture the negatives?

choice was correct
Why

The one substantive decision never tested during the project: LCM was fit to the precision (the GGN) throughout, whereas the paper fits it to a covariance. If that were backwards, every negative above would be suspect.

Outcome

Fitting the precision wins on every metric, and the alternative is worse than diagonal on both predictive measures. (Fitting the covariance does win on raw covariance Frobenius error — 0.595 vs 0.785, it captures the large-variance directions better — but that does not become posterior or predictive quality.)

Conclusion

The choice made was the right one. Had the alternative been chosen, the results would have been more negative, not less.

AUDIT 4

Would a larger model or a compute cluster help?

no — structural
Why

The practical question behind any negative result: is this a resource limit that scale would fix?

What

A linear layer's GGN is exactly Kronecker (Λ ⊗ ΦΦᵀ) — KFAC's home, and something a bandwidth-1 chain cannot represent except degenerately. Measured the nearest Kronecker product against the best chain on real trained last-layer curvature, at two widths.

Outcome

The gap widens with scale. Growing the layer improved the Kronecker fit (0.530 → 0.451) while the chain stayed flat (0.733 → 0.709). Corroborating: the chain fit to a real GGN plateaus at ~0.66 regardless of ordering, nugget, or optimisation budget (0.6585 at 800 steps → 0.6582 at 1600), while the same fitter recovers a genuine chain to 1.4e-4.

Conclusion

Scaling up makes KFAC relatively better, not LCM. This is a representational mismatch, not a resource limit — compute is the one intervention that provably cannot work here.

AUDIT 5

Was LCM applied to the wrong target — weights instead of features?

no evidence, with a caveat
Why

The paper applies LCM to conv feature maps (spatially correlated, naturally raster-orderable); this project applied it to weight curvature. A plausible explanation for the whole negative arc.

Outcome

Fitting an actual conv feature-map covariance (8×14×14, D=1568): LCM 0.924 vs diagonal 0.967 — barely better, and relatively worse than on weight curvature (0.66 vs 0.97).

Conclusion

“Wrong target” does not explain the negatives. Caveat, stated plainly: this test used an untrained network with n = 512 < D = 1568, so the empirical covariance is rank-deficient — a regime Exp 9 showed LCM fits poorly. The same caveat applies to the paper, whose feature dimension reaches ~2·10⁵ with far smaller batches; it too operates where absolute fit quality is poor and only the comparison against diagonal is meaningful. Consistent with, not contradictory to, the findings here.

What the audit did not rule out

Formulation B was tested at one setting, not across the campaign. The habitat test is confounded by rank deficiency and an untrained network. Some early experiments used 2–3 seeds (the headline joint-UQ result was re-run at 10), and only two real datasets were used. None of these affect the two structural findings — the formulation comparison and the Kronecker argument — which are the load-bearing ones.

Honest accounting

Limitations

  1. Narrow niche. The only demonstrated advantage is joint predictive UQ for variables with a known 1-D order and decaying correlation, at n ≳ d. Not neural-network weights (KFAC dominates), not marginal calibration or OOD, not low-data tasks.
  2. The learned coordinate is inert. It cannot recover unknown orders and is unnecessary when the order is known — undercutting the novelty over classical banded-GMRF and state-space-GP models.
  3. Extreme ordering fragility. Needs ≳0.999 accuracy; a mis-ordered chain is worse than a diagonal.
  4. Effect size is modest. The 10-seed joint-UQ gap is 62%/52%/23% with wide intervals, shrinking as d grows, and not significant at d = 500.
  5. Implementation scaling. Wall-clock grows ~O(d^1.44), not O(d log d): the blocked kernel's sequential block count grows with the coordinate range. The asymptotic work is O(d); the implementation is not.
  6. Statistical scope. Two real datasets × 5 splits; heuristic noise in the real-data pass; 2–3 seeds for some early experiments (headline result re-run at 10).
  7. Seed variance. In one of three fragility seeds, diagonal actually beat the exact full posterior — the premise itself is noisy at these sizes.
Where this leaves the work

Publication assessment

Publishable — as an honest characterisation plus a systems contribution, not as a new state-of-the-art method. Recommended path: an approximate-inference workshop (AABI / NeurIPS-BDL) first, then TMLR, whose bar is supported claims rather than impact. A main-conference submission is out of reach without new methodology.

What can be claimed

  • A near-linear structured covariance for Laplace approximations (3d parameters, O(d)–O(d log d) primitives, an associative-scan log-determinant 15–64× faster than the sequential version) shipped in a maintained library with tests.
  • A careful characterisation of when structured covariance helps: joint not marginal; chain not Kronecker; needs n ≳ d and a near-exact ordering.
  • A real-data demonstration that it recovers full-Laplace joint uncertainty for known-1-D-ordered features.

What must not be claimed

  • State-of-the-art Bayesian deep-learning uncertainty.
  • Any active-learning or Bayesian-optimisation gain — demonstrated failure.
  • A working learned ordering.
  • Novelty of the joint-vs-marginal insight — that is Osband et al.'s established thesis (Epistemic Neural Networks, The Neural Testbed), which this work uses as motivation and evaluation framework, not as a contribution.
The objection that is now conceded

“If the order is known, just band it.” That is essentially correct: in its working regime LCM is a banded / state-space-GP precision with a faster log-determinant. The paper must concede this and differentiate on the systems contribution, not on the covariance family.

Concrete follow-ups, ranked by expected value, are in the next section.

Where to go next

Follow-ups — what to do, why, and what it would buy

Ranked by expected value, not by ease. Each entry states the gap it addresses, what it would give if it works, and an honest effort / risk read. The tiers matter: Tier 1 is what stands between this work and a submission; Tier 2 are research bets that could genuinely change the conclusions; Tier 3 improves the artifact but will not move any result. The last group is what not to spend on — knowing that is worth as much as the rest.

Tier 1 · Required to publish
F1

More real known-order datasets

highest value
Gap

The entire positive finding rests on one real dataset. Tecator NIR closed ~100% of the diagonal→full joint-LL gap; the second real dataset (CO₂) was null because n ≫ d left nothing to win. One dataset is not a regime, and no reviewer will accept it.

What it gives

Converts “LCM helped on Tecator” into “LCM helps for ordered-feature models with n ≳ d” — i.e. an empirical claim with a stated scope. This single item is the difference between a workshop note and a defensible TMLR submission.

How

Target data with a genuine 1-D order and n ≳ d: NIR/Raman chemometrics beyond Tecator, mass-spectrometry, genomics with linkage-disequilibrium structure, climate or soil transects. Use marginal-likelihood-tuned noise (not the heuristic used in the first pass) and ≥10 seeds. Pre-register the prediction: marginal LL ties diagonal, joint LL beats it.

Effort / risk

Low effort — the harness already exists. Moderate risk: it may simply not replicate, in which case the honest paper is a pure negative result. That is a real possible outcome and should be accepted in advance.

F2

Fix the O(d^1.44) scaling with an associative-scan block carry

high value, low risk
Gap

The systems contribution is the least disputable part of this work — and its headline claim is currently untrue in wall-clock. The blocked kernel's sequential block count grows with the coordinate range, so measured cost scales O(d^1.44), not O(d log d).

What it gives

Makes the one unambiguous contribution actually hold end-to-end, and removes the most obvious attack on it. It also compounds: every fit, every experiment at large d, gets cheaper, which makes F1 and F5 practical.

How

The block-carry recursion is a first-order linear recurrence with coefficients ≤ 1 — exactly the form already solved by the 2×2 associative scan written for the log-determinant. Reuse that machinery instead of the sequential Python loop over blocks.

Effort / risk

Low–moderate effort; the machinery exists and is tested. Low risk — this is engineering with a known solution, and correctness is checkable against the dense kernel.

Tier 2 · Research bets that could change the conclusions
F3

Higher-order Matérn (3/2, 5/2) — attack the fragility at its root

most interesting bet
Gap

The finding that kills the method's novelty is the ordering fragility: ≥0.999 accuracy required, 0.998 already fails. The cause is bandwidth-1 exponential decay — adjacent coordinates are asserted to be strongly correlated, so a single misplacement injects a large false correlation. Every other limitation follows from this one.

What it gives

A Matérn-3/2 or 5/2 kernel has a banded (bandwidth 2–3) precision and smoother sample paths, so a misplaced coordinate is penalised far less. If that lowers the tolerance from 0.999 to, say, 0.99, then seriation becomes viable — and with it the entire learned/recovered-ordering story reopens, along with the unknown-order applications currently closed. This is the highest-leverage single change in the whole project: it attacks the root cause rather than working around it.

How

Matérn half-integer kernels have standard linear state-space representations, so the Kalman/associative-scan machinery generalises directly — the state dimension grows from 1 to 2 or 3. Re-run the fragility sweep (Exp 10b) per kernel order and read off the new threshold.

Effort / risk

Moderate effort. Medium risk: smoother kernels may simply fit real curvature worse, trading fragility for bias. But the experiment is cheap and the answer is decisive either way — run the fragility sweep before committing to anything downstream.

F4

Kronecker × chain hybrid — use LCM inside KFAC's factors

the audit points here
Gap

The audit established two facts that have not yet been combined: (i) neural-network layer curvature is Kronecker (Λ ⊗ ΦΦᵀ), which is why a global chain fails; (ii) the paper's own success is on feature maps, which do have spatial 1-D structure. But ΦΦᵀ — KFAC's second factor — is a feature covariance.

What it gives

Keep Kronecker for the layer structure (where it demonstrably belongs) and use LCM for the within-factor feature covariance (where the paper showed chains belong). For a wide layer whose input factor is in × in, that replaces a dense factor with 3·in parameters — a cheaper KFAC rather than a worse one. It is the only route identified that could make LCM useful for neural networks, and it is a genuinely novel combination rather than a re-derivation of banded GMRFs.

How

Fit LCM to KFAC's input factor (and optionally the output factor), using raster/channel order as the initialisation for conv layers. Compare against full KFAC on KL-to-full and predictive metrics, at matched memory.

Effort / risk

Moderate–high effort. Medium risk: the audit's quick feature-map test was unflattering (0.924 vs diagonal 0.967), though confounded by rank deficiency. Test the factor-fit quality first — it is a cheap go/no-go before building anything.

F5

Functional / GP-space Laplace — change what the variables are

scoping experiment
Gap

Every experiment here placed LCM in weight space, which the audit showed is the wrong habitat by construction. The untested alternative: make the correlated variables data points rather than weights.

What it gives

For data with a natural 1-D index — time series, spectra, spatial transects — the covariance over points is genuinely Matérn-like, so LCM is exactly the right model rather than an approximation, and gives a linear-time GP posterior. It also plugs into the functional Laplace already in the library (hessian_structure="gp").

Effort / risk

Moderate effort. High novelty risk: this is classical state-space-GP territory (Hartikainen & Särkkä; Lindgren–Rue–Lindström), so the contribution would be integration and systems, not a new model. Worth one scoping experiment to size the win before committing — do not build it out first.

Tier 3 · Improves the artifact, will not change any conclusion
F6

Matrix-free LCMLaplace end-to-end

engineering
Gap

The class materialises the dense GGN, so the O(P) scalability holds for the primitives but not for the class, which is capped at moderate P.

What it gives

A structured Laplace usable at P ≥ 10⁵ as a product rather than a component — the difference between “the maths scales” and “the tool scales”. Valuable for the library and for anyone building on it.

Effort / risk

Moderate–high effort, low technical risk. But be clear-eyed: it will not change a single scientific conclusion, because weight-space is the wrong habitat regardless of how large it gets. Do this for the artifact, not for the paper.

F7

Rank-aware fitting for the n ≪ d regime

may be fundamental
Gap

The fit degrades badly when curvature is low-rank — residual 0.13 at n=3000 versus 0.74 at n=40 — which is precisely why batch active learning failed, and why the paper's own n ≪ D regime yields poor absolute fits.

What it gives

Would reopen the low-data applications currently closed: batch active learning, Bayesian optimisation, sequential design — the natural consumers of joint uncertainty. It would also make comparisons against the paper's regime fairer.

Effort / risk

Moderate effort. High risk, and possibly fundamental: a rank-40 target may simply not contain enough information to identify 3d parameters, in which case no objective fixes it. Note also that Exp 9's failure may have had two causes — fit quality and greedy selection — so isolate them before investing.

Do not spend here
Bigger models or a compute cluster

Why not: the audit measured the Kronecker-vs-chain gap widening with layer width (0.530 → 0.451 for Kronecker while the chain stayed at 0.733 → 0.709). The mismatch is representational, so scale makes KFAC relatively better, not LCM. What you would get: a more expensive confirmation of a result already in hand. This is the one intervention that provably cannot work.

Better ordering recovery (seriation, discrete search, learned embeddings)

Why not — conditionally. The requirement is ≥0.999 accuracy; measured recovery is 0.31–0.99 for spectral seriation (mean 0.67) and ≈0.03 for gradient learning. Even an excellent ordering falls short, so effort spent here is wasted at the current kernel bandwidth. This is gated on F3: if a higher-order Matérn lowers the threshold to ~0.99, this becomes one of the most valuable directions instead of one of the worst. Run F3 first; let its result decide.

Suggested order

F2 (cheap, unblocks everything) → F3 (cheap, decisive, and gates the ordering question) → F1 (determines whether there is a paper) → then F4 or F5 depending on whether F3 reopened the neural-network case. F6 and F7 only if the library itself is the goal.
